Doubles positioning for new pickleball players

Most early doubles confusion is positional, not technical. Know who should be back, who can move forward, and how to travel with your partner before trying advanced patterns.

Published 19 August 2026Beginner guidePractice, not a rulebook

First principle

Move as a pair.

Two players who move together leave fewer accidental gaps than two players chasing separate balls.

Try the first drill ↗
The simple picture

The serving team starts back because it must let the return bounce. The returner can move forward after the return. The returner’s partner is usually already near the kitchen line. That sequence—not a clever formation—is the foundation of doubles positioning.

After the serve

Serve, then stay behind the baseline long enough to play the required return bounce. Your partner should also be prepared to let the return bounce on your side. The receiving team has an early opportunity to establish position near the non-volley zone, but neither player should rush blindly into a ball at their feet.

The double-bounce rule is why the two teams do not begin a rally in identical positions. Review the basic rules if that sequence is not yet automatic.

The kitchen line is a working position, not a destination

When your team can safely move forward, stand near the non-volley-zone line with enough space to react. Do not plant your feet on the line and reach for every ball. A ball driven at the body, a lob or a shot behind you may require a step back. The goal is balance and a clear view, not touching a painted line.

How partners should move

  • When one player moves left for a ball, the partner shifts left enough to protect the middle.
  • When one player is pulled wide, the partner avoids staying fixed in the opposite corner.
  • Call “mine”, “yours” or “leave” early and plainly; silence creates more errors than imperfect technique.
  • After a difficult shot, recover toward a position beside your partner rather than watching the ball.

A useful first drill

Without hitting a ball, place two cones just inside each sideline at the kitchen line. One partner points to a side; both players shuffle together two steps in that direction, then return to centre. Repeat until neither player crosses in front of the other. Add gentle dinks only after the movement feels natural.

Leave stacking, poaching systems and detailed hand signals for later. A team that understands the return sequence, communicates and moves together will solve the majority of beginner doubles problems.
