Pickleball faults explained

A fault is a rule violation that stops play. In standard side-out scoring, a receiving-team fault gives the serving team a point; a serving-team fault costs the server the serve or produces a side out.

Published 18 August 20262026 rules checkedStandard side-out scoring

The useful question

Who faulted?

That usually tells you the consequence. Receiving side faults: point to server. Serving side faults: service turn ends or moves to the partner.

See the common faults ↗
Do not overcomplicate it

Most beginner disputes fall into a short list: illegal serve, wrong service box, two-bounce violation, ball out, double bounce, kitchen volley or touching the net while the ball is live.

What counts as a fault?

USA Pickleball’s current rules summary defines a fault as an action that stops play because of a rule violation. The summary specifically includes service violations, balls hit into the net or out, volleying before the required bounce on each side, volleying from the non-volley zone, allowing two bounces, touching the net while the ball is in play and being struck by the live ball.

What happens after a fault?

Receiving team faults
Under standard side-out scoring, the serving side scores a point.
Serving team faults
The server loses the serve; in doubles this may pass to the partner or become a side out.
Rally scoring event
Do not use the side-out consequence automatically; follow the event’s published scoring rules.
Unsure of format?
Confirm the event rules before play. “Fault” and “who scores” are separate questions.

The faults you will see most often

1. The serve misses the correct service court

The serve has to travel diagonally into the opposite service court. A serve that lands outside that court is a fault.

Remember that serving rules are their own topic. A serve can also fault because of illegal foot position, illegal volley-serve mechanics or an improper release. See our 2026 serving guide.

2. The ball is hit into the net

If your shot does not cross the net and remain in play, the rally is over. On a serve, the current USA Pickleball framework gives you one serve attempt; do not assume a tennis-style second serve.

3. The return is volleyed before the two-bounce requirement is complete

The receiving side must let the serve bounce. Then the serving side must let the return bounce. Only after those two required bounces have occurred—one on each side—may either side volley.

The name causes confusion: it does not mean the ball has to bounce twice on one side.

4. The ball lands out

A ball that lands outside the court is out. Lines matter: a ball touching a boundary line is generally treated as in, while the non-volley-zone line has a special role on a serve because the serve must clear the non-volley zone.

5. You volley from the kitchen

The non-volley zone—usually called the kitchen—is not a no-entry zone. It is a no-volley zone.

You can stand in it. You can enter it. You can hit a ball after it bounces there. The fault occurs when you volley while touching the non-volley zone or its line, or when your volley momentum takes you into it. Our kitchen explainer covers the momentum rule in detail.

6. The ball bounces twice before you return it

In ordinary play, letting the ball bounce twice on your side before you strike it ends the rally. Do not confuse this with the two-bounce rule at the start of each rally.

7. You touch the net while the ball is live

If a player, clothing or paddle touches the net or net post while the ball is in play, that is a fault under the current rules summary.

That includes the paddle you are holding. “I did not touch it with my body” is not a defence if the paddle made contact while the ball was still live.

8. The live ball hits you or something you are wearing

A ball in play striking a player—or something the player is wearing or carrying—is a fault. This is why stepping aside from an out-bound ball can matter: if you touch it before it is dead, you may change the outcome.

9. The ball hits a permanent object before bouncing in court

The current USA Pickleball summary also lists a live ball striking a permanent object before bouncing on the court as a fault. In real venues, overhead structures, walls, lights and fencing can make local play messy; tournament or venue rules may add procedures for unusual obstructions.

Kitchen faults: the three situations beginners mix up

  1. You stand in the kitchen and the ball bounces before you hit it: legal.
  2. You stand in the kitchen and hit the ball out of the air: fault.
  3. You volley outside the kitchen but your momentum carries you in: fault.

The third one is the trap. Your position at the instant of paddle contact is not the whole test; the momentum from the volley matters.

Serving faults: the short version

A serving fault can come from either the delivery or the result.

  • Volley serve does not satisfy the current upward-arc / waist / paddle-head requirements.
  • Drop serve release is improperly propelled or manipulated.
  • Server foot position is illegal at contact.
  • Serve lands outside the correct diagonal service court.
  • Wrong player or position rules are violated under the applicable format.

Because service rules have changed over time, avoid using an undated social post or an old association explainer as your only source.

What is not automatically a fault?

  • Standing in the kitchen. Legal unless you volley or violate the momentum rule.
  • Hitting a ball after it bounces in the kitchen. Legal.
  • Applying spin with the paddle at serve contact. The 2026 clarification permits spin at paddle contact; pre-spin manipulation on release is the issue.
  • Using a drop serve. Legal under the current USA Pickleball rules.
  • Winning a rally while receiving. In standard side-out scoring it wins the serve, not a point; that is scoring, not a fault question.

A simple way to settle recreational disputes

Do not argue from memory. Ask three things:

  1. What happened? Describe the action, not the conclusion.
  2. Which rule category applies? Serve, bounce, non-volley zone, net, line or player contact?
  3. What scoring system are we using? Standard side-out or an event-specific rally-scoring format?

That separates the rule violation from its scoring consequence and usually resolves the disagreement quickly.
